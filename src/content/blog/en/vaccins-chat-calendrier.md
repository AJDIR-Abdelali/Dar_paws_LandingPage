"My cat never goes out, does it really need vaccines?" It's the most common question. The answer is **yes**: some diseases come into the home on your shoes or with another animal. Here is the usual schedule, which your vet adapts to your cat.

## Core vaccines for cats

According to international veterinary guidelines ([WSAVA](https://wsava.org/global-guidelines/vaccination-guidelines/)), all cats should be protected against:

- **Panleukopenia (feline distemper)**: highly contagious and often fatal in kittens. The virus survives a long time in the environment.
- **Cat flu**: mainly caused by herpesvirus and calicivirus. Sneezing, runny eyes, fever, mouth ulcers.

Depending on lifestyle, the vet may also recommend:

- **Feline leukaemia (FeLV)**: for cats that go out or live with other cats.
- **Rabies**: required to travel abroad, and recommended for cats that go out, as rabies is present in Morocco.

## The kitten schedule

| Age | Usual vaccines |
|---|---|
| 8 weeks | 1st injection panleukopenia + cat flu |
| 12 weeks | 2nd injection (+ leukaemia if needed) |
| 16 weeks | sometimes a 3rd injection, depending on the vet |
| 1 year | booster |

> Deworm your kitten and get a check-up before its first vaccine: it must be healthy on the day.

## Adult boosters

- **Panleukopenia**: often every three years after the one-year booster, depending on the vaccine.
- **Cat flu**: often every year, especially if the cat goes to boarding or meets other cats.
- **Leukaemia**: depending on the risk.
- **Rabies**: depending on the vaccine, every year or every three years.

## Indoor cats

A cat that never goes out has fewer risks, but not zero:

- the panleukopenia virus can be **carried in on your shoes**;
- a **stay at a cattery** or with a sitter caring for other cats exposes it to cat flu;
- **escapes** through a window or balcony happen more often than you think.

The vet can adapt the schedule: fewer vaccines, but the core ones remain important.

## Before leaving your cat with someone

If you travel and your cat goes to a sitter or cattery, check its **vaccines are up to date at least two weeks before**. Many sitters ask for it, and it protects your cat.

## After the vaccine

Your cat may be a little tired or eat less for 24 hours. Call the vet in case of vomiting, a swollen face, breathing difficulties, or a lump at the injection site that persists for weeks.

## Street cats and adopted cats: special cases

In Morocco, many cats are **adopted from the street** or taken in very young. In that case, nobody knows their exact age or medical history. Here's the recommended approach:

1. **Vet visit** in the first days, to estimate age and check general health.
2. **Treatment** against worms and fleas before any vaccination.
3. **Keep it apart** for a few days if you already have other cats, until you're sure it isn't sick.
4. **Full first vaccination**: two injections a few weeks apart, then the booster.
5. **Neutering** at the age your vet recommends.

If you feed the cats in your building, know that some associations run **neutering and vaccination** programmes for street cats. It's a practical way to reduce disease in the neighbourhood and also to protect your own cats, especially if they go out on the terrace or balcony.

A healthy, vaccinated, neutered cat can live 15 years or more. The few appointments of the first year are a small investment for many years together.

## FAQ

**At what age can a kitten be vaccinated?**
Usually from 8 weeks.

**My adopted adult cat has no record, what now?**
The vet restarts a full schedule: two injections a few weeks apart, then boosters.

**Can a vaccine make my cat ill?**
Serious side effects are rare. The benefits far outweigh the risks.
